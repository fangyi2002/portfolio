Making Michigan’s small claims form easier to understand

Helping people navigate a legal form without legal training.

I led a five-person student research team studying how people without legal backgrounds completed Michigan’s small claims form. Through 12 usability sessions, we identified four recurring challenges and developed recommendations for clearer language, instructions, and structure.

01 / The challenge

A legal form for people without legal training

The small claims form asks people to explain their dispute using language and procedures they may never have encountered before. The version we studied included a three-page form and a separate six-page instruction packet.

Our task was to understand where people struggled and what the form assumed they already knew.

How easy is this form to understand and complete?

  • Where do people encounter difficulties, and how do they respond?
  • How do people without legal backgrounds interpret the terminology?

02 / My contribution

Guiding the research from planning to synthesis

I helped turn our partner’s concerns into a focused research plan and coordinated the team’s work across the five-week project.

I led discussions to define our questions and testing approach, developed testing materials with teammates, participated in usability sessions, and facilitated synthesis. During analysis, I helped the team move from individual observations to broader patterns and recommendations.

03 / Research approach

Watching people complete a realistic task

We conducted 20–30-minute, in-person sessions with 12 participants. None had a legal background or had previously completed a small claims form.

Participants received the form, its instructions, and a fictional scenario: a landlord had not returned a $1,500 security deposit. They completed the form while explaining their thinking aloud.

The same scenario helped us compare interpretations of the same information. Think-aloud testing revealed where participants hesitated, guessed, or looked for help. Follow-up questions explored their experience and understanding of the next steps.

We also worked with our partner to develop an expected response, allowing us to compare participants’ interpretations with what the form required.

04 / Synthesis

Connecting individual struggles to broader patterns

We brought together observations from the sessions and grouped them through affinity mapping. I facilitated discussions about what the observations meant and how they related to our research questions.

We compared participants’ first impressions with what happened during completion. The form often appeared straightforward at first, but dense instructions made it harder to find guidance as questions arose.

Four themes emerged: unfamiliar terminology, dense instructions, unclear section responsibilities, and confusion that increased as participants progressed.

05 / Findings

Confidence did not always mean understanding

01Unfamiliar terms led to guesses.

Participants struggled with terms such as “plaintiff,” “next friend,” and “sole proprietor.” When they could not interpret a term, they sometimes guessed what information belonged in the field.

“I guess I would be the plaintiff, right?”

We recommended: replacing jargon with everyday language where possible, defining necessary terms beside the relevant field, and adding short examples of expected responses.

02Dense instructions were difficult to use.

Participants described the instructions as long and overwhelming. Dense blocks of text discouraged rereading, making the packet difficult to use as a reference.

“I feel like they make sense, but there is so much information.”

We recommended: shorter, clearly labeled sections, prioritizing essential information, and connecting guidance directly to the action users needed to take.

03Users could not tell which sections were theirs to complete.

The layout did not consistently distinguish users’ responsibilities from sections intended for court staff or a notary. Participants were unsure whether to complete certain areas, and some fields offered too little writing space.

We recommended: separating sections by responsibility, providing adequate space, and ordering fields so their purpose was easier to follow.

04Early confidence gave way to confusion and fatigue.

The form often looked straightforward at first. As participants progressed, unfamiliar terms and repeated movement between the form and instructions created confusion. Some remained confident despite making errors.

We recommended: placing guidance near the relevant fields, improving the information hierarchy, and reducing the information users needed to interpret at each step.

A form that looks easy can still be hard to complete.

Participants’ confidence did not reliably indicate whether they understood the form correctly. We needed to consider both what people said about the experience and what they actually did.

06 / Outcome

Providing user evidence to support an ongoing redesign

Our study identified four recurring usability challenges and translated them into practical recommendations. We presented our findings to Civil Legal Design and city staff.

Civil Legal Design was already redesigning the form when we conducted the study. Our client shared that the insights were useful and confirmed the direction of the ongoing redesign.

Research findingRecommended direction
Unfamiliar terminologyEveryday language and nearby definitions
Dense instructionsShort, scannable guidance tied to actions
Unclear responsibilitiesDistinct sections and logical field order
Growing confusion and fatigueGuidance within the flow and clearer hierarchy

We also identified questions for future research, including how people find the instructions and understand the broader filing process.

07 / Reflection

The same form can serve different users—and serve them unevenly.

The small claims form serves people filing a case, court clerks, and judges. What feels clear to someone familiar with the legal system may confuse someone encountering it for the first time.

This project taught me to ask “easy for whom?” when defining a usability problem. Understanding who uses a product, what they already know, and what they need from it is essential to asking the right research questions.

Leading the team helped me practice making room for different interpretations, returning to the evidence, and helping the group reach a shared understanding.

What I would explore next

Our scenario gave us a consistent task, but could not fully capture the experience of someone managing a real dispute. Providing the instruction packet also meant we did not test how people found it independently.

With more time, I would test proposed revisions and explore the full journey—from finding the form and instructions to understanding where and how to file.
